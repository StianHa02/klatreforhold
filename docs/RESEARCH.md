# Turning open weather data into dry-rock forecasts

Whether Ekstraveggen is wet or dry can be predicted from public data alone. The prediction comes from a small physics model that tracks a sub-millimetre water film, not from a humidity threshold, and the club's first version already has the right structure. Gneiss and granite hold almost no water inside the stone, so wetness on the wall comes from a thin surface film, from water in cracks and vegetation, and from seepage. Each of these behaves on a different timescale, from hours to weeks. Road-weather models (METRo, RoadSurf) and leaf-wetness models already handle the same bookkeeping, and their published parameters give good starting values. MET Nordic, MET Norway's free 1 km hourly analysis, is the only free source that supplies every input, including downwelling longwave radiation, which matters for condensation. The biggest weakness is not the physics or the data. **There is no ground truth for any natural rock face, let alone this one.** The wind-driven-rain coefficient, the film capacity and the seepage threshold are all borrowed from buildings, roads or crops. The highest-value next step is therefore a structured wet/dry log from local climbers, ideally with a time-lapse camera, and then calibrating five to eight parameters against it. Several API details in this report come from documentation and were not tested live during the research, because the research environment could not reach MET's servers. These are flagged where they matter.

## A wet gneiss wall is a 0.1 mm film problem, not a sponge problem

The first question is where the water sits, because that sets how long the wall stays wet. Crystalline rock is extremely tight. A certified Finnish grey granite has **open porosity of 0.5% and water absorption of 0.2% by mass** ([Kurun Kivi datasheet](https://kivi.info/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Kuru-Grey-Kurun-Kivi-Oy-2023-EN.pdf)), and dense granites and diorites range from 0.2% to 0.6% porosity, against 2–3% for intermediate limestones ([Stone World](https://www.stoneworld.com/articles/82270-granite-limestone-and-marble-part-i-structural-and-chemical-effects-on-water-absorption)). Porous limestone, by contrast, soaks up about **16% water by weight and takes hundreds of hours to dry** ([LNEC drying course](https://repositorio.lnec.pt/bitstream/123456789/1004187/1/TG1%20-%20DRYING%20-%20v3%20-%20Charisma%20course%202012.pdf)). Put the granite numbers in water terms: at 0.2% absorption and 2,620 kg/m³, wetting the outer 1–5 mm of rock stores only about **0.005–0.03 mm of water**. That is too little to make holds slippery, though enough to keep the rock looking dark. The water climbers notice sits on the surface. Building scientists measured about **0.08 mm of adhesion water retained per rain spell** on a smooth vertical gauge surface ([Blocken & Carmeliet 2004](https://aecb.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Blocken_Carmeliet_A-review-of-wind-driven-rain-research-in-building-science-2.pdf)). This matches v1's 0.1 mm film store well. The 0.08 mm figure comes from a gauge, not rough rock, so the true capacity is somewhat higher in pockets, crystals and crack mouths.

The rock type at Bønes is not confirmed. The Bergen Klatreklubb guide says only that "Fjellet er fast" (the rock is solid) ([BKK](https://ute.bergenklatreklubb.no/artikler/maanedens-klatrefelt-ekstraveggen)). Regional geology places the Bergen arcs' mica schist and limestone belt near Nordåsvatnet, with gneisses in other arcs ([SNL: Bergens geologi](https://snl.no/Bergens_geologi)). If the wall turns out to be schist, water held along the foliation would make the crack/holds store more important. One query to NGU's bedrock map service settles it ([Berggrunn WMS3](https://inspire-geoportal.ec.europa.eu/srv/api/records/51243d4e-e86e-474b-baf8-b7ccfb59fe8e)).

### Rain reaches a vertical wall through the wind, and the coefficient is uncertain

Rain falling straight down barely touches a vertical face. Wall wetting is governed by wind-driven rain (WDR): R_wdr = κ·U·R_h, where U is wind speed and R_h is horizontal rainfall. Hoppestad's measured free-field coefficients are **Oslo 0.130, Bergen 0.188, Trondheim 0.221 and Tromsø 0.148 s/m**. Lacy's widely used general relation is R_wdr = 0.222·U·R_h^0.88, which corresponds to drops about 1.2 mm across. Drop size matters a great deal. Straube and Burnett give **above 0.5 s/m for drizzle and about 0.1 s/m for cloudbursts**, because small drops follow the wind ([Blocken & Carmeliet 2004](https://aecb.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Blocken_Carmeliet_A-review-of-wind-driven-rain-research-in-building-science-2.pdf)). On real walls the projected form is R_wdr = α·U·R_h·cosθ, with θ the angle between the wind direction and the wall normal. α ranges from **0.02 to 0.26 s/m depending on position**: top corners and edges catch the most, and the middle of large faces very little. The same review calls it a common error to use the free-field coefficient for a building ([Blocken & Carmeliet 2004](https://aecb.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Blocken_Carmeliet_A-review-of-wind-driven-rain-research-in-building-science-2.pdf)). This bears directly on v1, which uses 0.188. That value is Bergen's free-field number and so is better treated as an upper bound than a site value. On the other hand, the European wall-index method multiplies by a topography factor of up to **1.36 at steep cliff crests** ([Blocken & Carmeliet 2004](https://aecb.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Blocken_Carmeliet_A-review-of-wind-driven-rain-research-in-building-science-2.pdf)). A small crag could therefore sit on either side of 0.188. ISO 15927-3 itself, which is built on BS 8104, warns that in mountainous areas "direct measurements of the rain impacting on building façades" should be made ([ISO 15927-3 preview](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/ISO/preview_ISO+15927-3-2009.pdf)).

For a 300° wall, only winds from roughly 210° through west to 30° give a positive cosθ. Two further cautions apply. First, using hourly rather than 10-minute data can cause **errors of up to 80% in WDR amounts** ([Blocken & Carmeliet 2004](https://aecb.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Blocken_Carmeliet_A-review-of-wind-driven-rain-research-in-building-science-2.pdf)). For a yes/no wet state with a capped store this matters much less, because a few millimetres of rain fill a 0.1 mm store regardless. Second, the guide describes the right side as "mindre bratt og til dels på sva" (less steep, partly slab) ([BKK](https://ute.bergenklatreklubb.no/artikler/maanedens-klatrefelt-ekstraveggen)). Slabs catch vertical rain and runoff from above whatever the wind direction, so a single wall-wide α will not fit both halves.

### Drying is fast in theory and slow in Bergen air

With so little water stored, practically all drying happens in the "constant-rate" stage, where evaporation is limited by the air's demand rather than by the stone ([LNEC](https://repositorio.lnec.pt/bitstream/123456789/1004187/1/TG1%20-%20DRYING%20-%20v3%20-%20Charisma%20course%202012.pdf)). Laboratory measurements at 20 °C in near-still air give free-water evaporation of **DR = 0.1568 − 0.0017·RH kg/m²·h**: linear in relative humidity, and therefore proportional to the vapour pressure deficit ([Diaz Gonçalves et al. 2015](https://repositorio.lnec.pt/bitstream/123456789/1007508/2/Diaz%20Gon%c3%a7alves%20et%20al%20%282015%29%20Evaporation%20from%20porous%20building%20materials%20and%20its%20cooling%20potential.pdf)). Applying that line to a 0.1 mm film shows why "it stopped raining hours ago" is a poor guide in Bergen.

| Air RH (20 °C, still air) | Evaporation (mm/h) | Time to dry a 0.1 mm film |
|---|---|---|
| 50% | 0.072 | about 1.4 h |
| 70% | 0.038 | about 2.6 h |
| 80% | 0.021 | about 4.8 h |
| 90% | 0.004 | about 26 h |

Wind shortens these times, and the lab tests did not measure how much. Cooler air lengthens them, because the same RH means less vapour deficit. The shape of the table is the lesson: **drying collapses as RH approaches 90%**, which is exactly the range Bergen air sits in for much of the year. Two effects pull in opposite directions. Rough stone has an evaporating area **1.22–2.88 times its flat area**, so it can dry faster than free water ([Diaz Gonçalves et al. 2015](https://repositorio.lnec.pt/bitstream/123456789/1007508/2/Diaz%20Gon%c3%a7alves%20et%20al%20%282015%29%20Evaporation%20from%20porous%20building%20materials%20and%20its%20cooling%20potential.pdf)). Wet surfaces also cool themselves: in Lisbon sun, wet materials ran **about 10–15 °C cooler than dry ones**, which cuts the vapour deficit at the surface (same source). This is why the drying term should use the computed rock surface temperature rather than air temperature, as v1 does. The guide's own experience fits: "Noen ruter tørker rimelig raskt etter regn, men vent gjerne en dag om det har regnet mye" (some routes dry fairly quickly after rain, but wait a day if it has rained a lot) ([BKK](https://ute.bergenklatreklubb.no/artikler/maanedens-klatrefelt-ekstraveggen)). That sentence is the closest thing to a calibration target that exists today.

### Sweating comes from cold rock meeting mild air, not from clear-night dew

Vertical faces collect little radiative dew. Camuffo and Giorio found up to **0.2 mm per clear night on sky-facing surfaces, but "very small" amounts on vertical ones**. They also showed that because of the stone's thermal inertia, **morning condensation reaches the same order as nocturnal dew**, and that the largest single-night amounts occur in autumn ([Camuffo & Giorio 2003](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1022893012277)). For Ekstraveggen the likely sweating scenario is a mild, humid south-westerly airstream arriving after a cold spell, with the dew point rising above a rock surface that is still cold. v1's surface energy balance with condensation is the right tool for this, provided the rock has enough thermal mass in the model to lag the air. Below the threshold of visible water, high surface humidity still hurts friction. Practitioner guidance says that "even a seemingly dry hold will be very wet after a couple of tries if the humidity is above 70 per cent" ([Gripped](https://gripped.com/?p=38685)). That is a heuristic rather than a measurement, but it supports a separate "damp/greasy" output class.

### Seepage and lichen are the slow reservoirs, and nobody has measured them

No source found gives seepage lag, duration or rainfall thresholds for any climbing crag. The closest evidence comes from masonry. After simulated driving rain on a limestone wall, drying followed **i = R·t^½, slowing markedly over 48 hours** ([Orr, Viles et al.](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10083126/1/Untitled.pdf)), which is the signature of a long-tailed store. Lichens such as *Verrucaria nigrescens* **retain moisture** and take up water strongly on colonised stone ([Morando et al.](https://iris.unito.it/retrieve/handle/2318/1622869/291450/Morando_et_al_ESPL_full_text_revision_4aperto.pdf)). Vegetated ledges and cracks therefore act as local sponges that stay wet after bare rock has dried. v1's slow seepage reservoir is the standard hydrological answer: a decaying antecedent-precipitation index driven by rain on the catchment above the wall. Its time constant and on/off thresholds must come from local observations, because nothing in the literature supplies them.

## Road-ice and leaf-wetness models already solved the bookkeeping

Three fields predict "is this surface wet?" every hour: road weather, plant disease and corrosion. Their experience points in the same direction. Calibrated physics beats raw humidity rules only when the surface differs from what the rule was fitted on. A vertical rock wall differs from everything those rules were fitted on.

| Analog | Core mechanism | Key published parameters | What transfers to Ekstraveggen |
|---|---|---|---|
| METRo (Environment Canada roads) | Energy balance plus liquid/ice water store | Runoff above **0.2 mm** at 3×10⁻³ s⁻¹; "wet" when store ≥ 0.2 mm; surface humidity blended from air to saturated over 0–0.5 mm ([METRo source](https://github.com/K0I05/METRo)) | Store, runoff and wet-state logic almost verbatim |
| FMI RoadSurf 1.1 | Energy balance, 16-layer ground, several stores | Surface water cap 1 mm; evaporation only when no ice and Ts ≥ 0.25 °C; sky-view factor and horizon shading; 4 h coupling to observed surface temperature ([Karsisto 2024](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/17/4837/2024/)) | Sky-view and horizon handling for radiation; nudging toward observations if a sensor is ever installed |
| Penman–Monteith leaf-wetness model | Signed latent heat flux plus capped store | Dew capacity 0.8 mm, rain store 0.6 mm; MAE 2.3–2.9 h/day ([Lulu et al. 2008](https://revistas.usp.br/sa/article/download/22384/24408/26016)) | Same structure as v1; most sensitive to net radiation and RH errors |
| RH and dew-point-depression rules | Thresholds on air humidity | RH ≥ 90%: fraction correct 0.87–0.92; **locally tuned thresholds reach MAE 1.23–1.89 h/day** ([Sentelhas et al. 2008](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/345929)) | Useful as a benchmark to beat, not as the model |
| ISO 9223 time-of-wetness | Air RH and temperature proxy | "Incomparable" with surface sensors in a coastal field test ([Schindelholz et al. 2013](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/comparability-and-accuracy-of-time-of-wetness-sensing-methods-rel/)) | Not suitable for a visible-water question |

Two results from this literature matter most for Bergen. First, a Dutch grassland test, the closest maritime analogue, found that a plain **87% RH rule under-predicted wetness (bias 0.70)** and that an "extended" rule using RH trends reached fraction correct 0.87 with bias 0.94 ([AMS conference paper](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/77740.pdf)). In damp climates, humidity rules miss wetting by rain and are slow to call drying. Second, the physical Penman–Monteith model did no better than calibrated rules on horizontal leaves, and its main error sources were **net radiation and RH accuracy** ([Lulu et al. 2008](https://revistas.usp.br/sa/article/download/22384/24408/26016)). The physics only pays for itself if radiation on the actual surface is done well. For a 300° wall that means geometry and shading, which humidity rules cannot represent at all. Heritage-stone research adds a conceptual warning: "time of wetness" has been used "by different groups to mean very different things", and surface wetness and interior wetness run on separate clocks ([McCabe et al. 2013](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/the-use-and-meanings-of-time-of-wetness-in-understanding-building/)). The project should define its target precisely, for example "visible water on holds of route X at hour t", before collecting labels.

METRo's surface-humidity rule is worth copying exactly, because it solves a subtle problem. The effective surface humidity is q_g = min(1, W/0.5)·q_sat(Ts) + max(0, 1 − W/0.5)·min(q_sat(Ts), q_a), where W is stored water in mm ([METRo source](https://github.com/K0I05/METRo)). A dry surface can therefore gain condensation but cannot evaporate water it does not have, and a partly wet surface evaporates at a reduced rate. RoadSurf's verification is a sobering note: across about 400 Finnish road stations, **observed water and ice states were judged too unreliable to evaluate against**, so only surface temperature was verified ([Karsisto 2024](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/17/4837/2024/)). Even professionals with sensors in the road struggle to verify wetness. A volunteer project should expect its labels to be noisy and plan for that.

## MET Nordic is the only free source with every input, including longwave

The input question has a clear answer. MET Nordic analyses on thredds.met.no provide 1 km hourly temperature, RH, 10 m wind and direction, cloud fraction, precipitation (accumulated over the preceding hour), and **both shortwave and longwave downwelling radiation as hourly integrals in W s m⁻²** ([NWPdocs: MET Nordic](https://github.com/metno/NWPdocs/wiki/MET-Nordic-dataset)). Longwave is what lets the model see overcast nights, which keep rock warm, against clear ones, which cool it toward the dew point. No other free source found supplies it at this resolution. The archive has a seam the team must handle. The rerun archive `metpparchivev4` covers **2012-09-01 to 2025-10-31**, analyses only. The operational `metpparchive` runs from 2018-03-01 onward and also holds forecasts issued at 00, 06, 12 and 18Z. The older v3 rerun should be treated as gone. The documentation contradicts itself on that point, but the two dates it gives (May 2026 and "no longer accessible") both put it out of reach today (same source). A long, NORA3-based "long-term-consistent" series reaches back to 1958 (same source).

| Source | Best use | Key facts and caveats |
|---|---|---|
| MET Nordic analyses (THREDDS) | Multi-year hindcast forcing | LCC grid `+proj=lcc +lat_0=63 +lon_0=15 +lat_1=63 +lat_2=63 +R=6.371e+06`; one file per hour, so roughly 8,760 OPeNDAP opens per year; divide radiation integrals by 3,600 to get W/m² ([NWPdocs](https://github.com/metno/NWPdocs/wiki/MET-Nordic-dataset)) |
| MET Nordic forecast files | Live forecast with longwave | `met_forecast_1_0km_nordic_latest.nc`, 58–64 h horizon (same source); whether forecast files contain longwave was not checked |
| Open-Meteo `metno_seamless` | Simple live JSON feed, tilted irradiance | MET Nordic for about 2.5 days, then ECMWF; GTI uses azimuth **0 = south, 90 = west**, a fixed 20% albedo and an isotropic sky; **no longwave** (`terrestrial_radiation` is top-of-atmosphere solar) ([Open-Meteo MET Norway](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/metno-api); [Satellite API](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/satellite-radiation-api)) |
| Open-Meteo Historical Forecast and Previous Runs | Easy history from 2022-11-15; lead-time-specific forecasts | Stitched first hours of each run; Previous Runs "mostly from January 2024" ([Historical Forecast](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-forecast-api); [Previous Runs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/previous-runs-api)) |
| Locationforecast 2.0 `complete` | Cross-check, dew point, cloud layers, precipitation probability | Hourly 0–60 h from MEPS 2.5 km; **no radiation**; identifying User-Agent required, coordinates at most 4 decimals, honour `Expires`/`If-Modified-Since` ([datamodel](https://docs.api.met.no/doc/locationforecast/datamodel); [ToS](https://docs.api.met.no/doc/TermsOfService)) |
| Nowcast 2.0 | "Is it raining at the crag now / in 2 h?" | Radar `precipitation_rate`, updated every 5 min, absent when `RadarCoverage` is not "ok" ([Nowcast doc](https://api.met.no/weatherapi/nowcast/2.0/documentation)) |
| Frost observations | Bias-correcting the grid; validation | Free client ID; hourly `air_temperature`, `relative_humidity`, `dew_point_temperature`, `sum(precipitation_amount PT1H)`, `mean(surface_downwelling_shortwave_flux_in_air PT1H)` ([Frost howto](https://frost.met.no/howto.html); [wetterdienst](https://wetterdienst.readthedocs.io/en/latest/data/provider/metno/frost/hourly.html)) |

Two conventions cause silent bugs. Open-Meteo's radiation values are **means over the preceding hour** ([Open-Meteo docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs)). MET Nordic precipitation is likewise an accumulation over the preceding hour, while its radiation integral window is not documented and should be checked in a file's metadata. If timestamps are misaligned by one hour, the 15:30–17:00 sun window lands in the wrong place. The azimuth conventions also differ. pvlib takes compass azimuth, so 300 for the wall. Open-Meteo's GTI takes south-referenced azimuth, so a 300° compass face becomes **120**, following the documented convention of 0 = south and 90 = west ([Open-Meteo MET Norway](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/metno-api)). The parameter name `azimuth` itself was not confirmed on a fetched page. On licensing, MET Norway data is NLOD 2.0 / CC BY 4.0 ("Data from MET Norway") ([api.met.no License](https://api.met.no/doc/License)). Open-Meteo's free tier is non-commercial only, which covers "personal or non-profit apps without ads or subscriptions", and is capped at 10,000 calls a day ([Open-Meteo Terms](https://open-meteo.com/en/terms)). A club tool fits within this. If the tool ever carries ads, the team should switch to MET's own endpoints.

### Geometry and nearby surfaces fill in what the weather grid cannot

The 1 km grid cannot see the wall, so geometry has to come from elevation data. Kartverket's national 1 m terrain and surface models (DTM1 and DOM1, the latter including trees and buildings) are **CC BY 4.0**, downloadable as GeoTIFF or 15 km tiles from an ATOM feed ([data.norge.no DTM1](https://data.norge.no/en/datasets/1a7327eb-1fa5-3432-8dea-fc198a5ede13/hoyde-dtm1)), or streamable via a seamless WCS whose metadata lists a more restrictive "Norge digitalt" licence ([Geonorge WCS record](https://geonorge.no/geonetworktest/srv/api/records/311782b2-26fe-4d1d-93e9-c90b45d5bd8b)). pvlib has no general terrain-horizon function, only row-to-row shading helpers ([pvlib docs](https://pvlib-python.readthedocs.io/en/stable/reference/generated/pvlib.irradiance.get_total_irradiance.html)). The team therefore has to cast rays from a point just off the wall foot to build a horizon profile, then zero the direct beam whenever the sun is below it. This is the same approach RoadSurf uses ([Karsisto 2024](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/17/4837/2024/)). A real discrepancy makes this worth doing. The brief says sun from about 15:30, while the club guide says "Veggen får sol sent på dagen, fra rundt kl 17" (the wall gets sun late in the day, from around 17:00) ([BKK](https://ute.bergenklatreklubb.no/artikler/maanedens-klatrefelt-ekstraveggen)). Trees in the surface model or the season could explain the 90-minute gap, and a DOM horizon will settle it.

For the closest thing to a measured surface near the crag, Statens vegvesen road-weather stations all measure road surface temperature. Only "a very few" measure surface condition. Their 10-minute data are archived by MET and "accessible through the Frost api" ([roadweather.met.no](https://roadweather.met.no/about)). Statens vegvesen's own DATEX II feed offers only current snapshots, behind registration, under NLOD ([Statens vegvesen DATEX](https://www.vegvesen.no/nn/fag/teknologi/data-og-api-ar/et-utvalg-apne-data/kva-er-datex/)). SN50710 "E39 Eidsvåg" is one such station in Bergen ([yr.no](https://yr.no/en/other-conditions/5-50710/Norway/Vestland/Bergen/E39%20Eidsv%C3%A5g)), and Bergen–Florida (SN50540) and Flesland (SN50500) are the main MET stations ([Frost examples](https://d73bbb8.frost.met.no/ex_userquest)). Which elements each station reports, and the exact element IDs for road surface temperature, must be confirmed with a Frost `availableTimeSeries` call. Asphalt is a horizontal, dark, sun-exposed surface, so road surface temperature is at best a rough check on the modelled rock temperature on cold, cloudy nights. Netatmo's public rain gauges could sharpen rain start and stop times, but they require an OAuth developer app with tokens that expire after three hours ([netatmo.weather](https://github.com/dimfalk/netatmo.weather)). NIBIO's leaf-wetness network may have Vestland stations, but nothing public confirms where they are or how to access the data ([lmt.nibio.no](https://lmt.nibio.no/)). No public webcam pointing at the crag was found.

## Calibration has to start with the club collecting its own labels

Every public dataset describes the air. None describes the rock. The model's handful of free parameters therefore cannot be fitted until someone records what the wall actually looked like. This is the central practical finding of the research. The literature supports a cheap approach. Leaf-wetness studies show that **locally calibrated simple models reach accuracy "close to errors reported for physical models"** ([Sentelhas et al. 2008](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/345929)), so a small number of local observations goes a long way. Heritage scientists have long instrumented stone surfaces with simple moisture sensors ([USGS WRI 87-4177](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/wri874177)), and a dated photo or a short climber report is enough to label an hour.

The plan that follows has four stages. **First, verify the plumbing.** Open one MET Nordic file over OPeNDAP and check the radiation window and the x/y units. Make one Frost `availableTimeSeries` call for SN50540, SN50500 and SN50710, one Frost element lookup, and one Open-Meteo call with `tilt=90`. Also run one NGU GetFeatureInfo for rock type and compute one DOM horizon profile. **Second, collect labels.** Set up a two-minute form for climbers (time, route or wall section, and one of dry / damp / wet / seeping, plus an optional photo). Add, if the club agrees, a time-lapse camera, since wet rock darkens visibly, and possibly a leaf-wetness or surface-resistance sensor with an IR thermometer reading for rock temperature. These target the three biggest unknowns: rock temperature lag, film drying time and the seepage threshold. **Third, hindcast and fit.** Run v1 over MET Nordic from 2012 onward and fit a small set of parameters by Bayesian inference against the labels. Score results with the same metrics the leaf-wetness literature uses (fraction correct, false alarm ratio, bias, and MAE in wet hours per day), so the numbers can be compared with published work. **Fourth, test in forecast mode** using archived MET Nordic forecasts or Open-Meteo Previous Runs, because a model tuned on analyses will look better than it performs on real 24-hour forecasts.

The suggested parameter set is about six to eight values, with priors anchored on the published ranges above. These are starting points chosen for this report, not sourced values:

| Parameter | Suggested prior | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| WDR coefficient α (s/m) | log-normal, median 0.12, spread ×1.6 | Facade range 0.02–0.26; Bergen free-field 0.188 as upper reference |
| Film capacity (mm) | log-normal, median 0.15–0.2 | Adhesion 0.08 mm; METRo 0.2 mm |
| "Wet" threshold (mm) | 0.05–0.5 mm | METRo 0.2 mm |
| Runoff rate (s⁻¹) | log-normal, median 3×10⁻³ | METRo |
| Evaporation multiplier | log-normal, median 1, spread ×1.6 | Rough-surface area 1.2–2.9×, no wind data |
| Seepage decay and on/off thresholds | Days to weeks; wide | Guide's "wait a day", no literature |
| Direct-rain fraction for slab sections | 0–0.3 | Right side partly slab |

Ascent records are a special kind of label. A logged climb implies the rock was dry enough, but a day without a climb says nothing. That is "positive-unlabelled" learning, and it is a well-studied problem ([Bekker & Davis 2020](https://mlanthology.org/mlj/2020/bekker2020mlj-learning)). The catch is that people climb on sunny weekends, not at random. Any use of ascent dates must account for the weekday, daylight and how pleasant the weather was, or the model will learn "nice weekend weather" instead of "dry rock". Once the hourly loop is written in a differentiable framework, the same parameters can be fitted with gradients, and a small neural correction can later be added on the evaporation or seepage terms. This "differentiable modelling" approach has shown better data efficiency than purely data-driven ML in geoscience ([Shen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04027v2)). It is worth doing only after the label count reaches the low hundreds.

## What to change in version 1

v1 already matches the consensus structure: a capped fast film, a slower store, ISO-style wind-driven rain, a surface energy balance with condensation, and plane-of-array irradiance. The recommended changes are about parameters, geometry and plumbing, not a rewrite.

Several items remain uncertain and should be treated as such. The exact ISO 15927-3 equations and wall-factor tables are paywalled and were not checked. No catch-ratio, drying-time or seepage measurement exists for any natural crystalline cliff. The rock type, the Frost element IDs and the live behaviour of every endpoint above were not verified. The WDR coefficient is the parameter most likely to be badly off, so it is the one to calibrate first.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. Physics and data are mostly solved problems: road-weather and leaf-wetness models provide a tested skeleton, and MET Nordic provides every forcing variable for free back to 2012. What decides the model's quality is a handful of site constants that no public source can provide: how much rain this wall catches, how much water its holds and cracks keep, and how long its seeps run. The lab drying figures also show why a naive model will mislead in Bergen. At 90% humidity a film that dries in about an hour at 50% can persist for about a day. Getting surface temperature, wind and sun right therefore matters more than getting rain totals right.

The practical implication for Bergen Klatreklubb is that the most valuable contribution from members is observations, not code. A season of honest "wet / damp / dry / seeping" reports, ideally backed by a time-lapse camera, would turn Ekstraveggen into one of very few natural rock faces anywhere with a calibrated wetness model. The same small parameter set would then transfer, with re-fitting, to other Bergen crags.
